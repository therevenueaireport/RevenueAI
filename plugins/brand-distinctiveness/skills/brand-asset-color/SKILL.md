# Distinctive asset: color

The hue recognized before a word is read. Color is meaning-free. It argues nothing.
It is pure retrieval: the odds the brand surfaces when the category comes up.

Color is the most-used distinctive asset and usually the highest-scoring across a
cohort, which is exactly its trap. When every brand in a category nails color, the
colors cancel. A category of distinct blues is still a category of blues. Score color
against the specific brands a buyer confuses this one with, never against the whole
world.

## Scoring, 0 to 3

- **0 Absent.** No consistent color, or all-neutral with no owned hue.
- **1 Category default.** A hue is used but it is the category's default (another SaaS
  blue, another security near-black, another fintech green). Present, not
  distinctive.
- **2 Distinctive in category.** An owned hue or color system, recognized inside the
  category before a word is read, applied consistently.
- **3 Out of context.** The hue alone triggers the brand anywhere, no name needed.

## The category-default trap

A 1 is a color that looks like the category. Most B2B blues live here. If three rivals
could run the same hue and nobody would blink, it is a 1 regardless of how carefully
it was chosen.

## Moving up the ladder

- **1 to 2:** Find the gap in the category's color space and take it. Commit to ONE
  primary hue, not a rainbow. Push saturation or pick an unexpected hue for the
  category. Apply it to close to 100% of the surface so the eye learns it. Picture a
  fictional payroll company in a category of navy and slate that takes a hot coral
  and puts it on every surface: site background, deck cover, booth wall, the lanyards.
  Buyers start calling it "the coral one" before they remember the name.
- **2 to 3:** Sustain that single hue, unbroken, across every surface for years until
  the color owns the brand out of context. Out-of-context recognition is earned by
  time plus discipline, not by a new palette.

## Build playbook

1. Pull the primary colors of the three to five brands the buyer confuses this one
   with. Map the category's color space.
2. Pick one hue in the gap. One. A distinctive primary beats a clever palette.
3. Write usage rules: where the primary appears, the minimum coverage, what never
   competes with it.
4. Apply everywhere at once. Color earns recognition through repetition and coverage,
   not through a brand guideline PDF.
5. Freeze it. Do not tune the hue every quarter.

## Hold it across the surface

Website, product UI, favicon and app icon, sales deck, one-pagers, email, social
avatars and post templates, paid ads, event booth, swag, slide masters. The same hue,
the same way, every time.

## Anti-patterns

- Adding the ninety-second distinct blue and calling it distinctive.
- A rainbow palette that dilutes the one hue that could have been owned.
- Refreshing the color. Recognition resets every time the hue moves.

## Strategic note

Color is crowded. Lift it to a clean 2 and stop. Do not pour the brand budget here.
The open goals are almost always [[brand-asset-sound]] and
[[brand-asset-human-identity]], with [[brand-asset-visual-device]] close behind. Send
the real investment there.
