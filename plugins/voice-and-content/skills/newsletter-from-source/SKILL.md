# Newsletter From Source

Turn source material into a newsletter issue that delivers executable intelligence. The bar is high on purpose: every issue should be valuable enough that a reader would pay for it, and a little surprised that they did not have to.

Two source families, two templates:

| Source | Template | The job |
|---|---|---|
| Podcast episode, interview, webinar, demo recording, transcript | **Source summary** (primary) | Extract what was shown and said, then make it executable |
| A set of news items, announcements, research releases | **News analysis** (secondary) | Find the pattern across them and say what to do |
| An episode that covers breaking news, or news that needs a demo to make sense | **Hybrid** | News context up top, tactical response from the episode below |

Full templates are in [references/newsletter-structures.md](references/newsletter-structures.md).

## Step 1: Understand the source and the intent

**For an episode, interview or transcript, gather:**

- Topic, guest (role and company type), and the reason this conversation matters now
- Any demos or screen shares: the exact workflow shown, the tools and settings used, the prompts or templates on screen
- The strategic points made, and where the guest disagreed with the common view
- Any results shared: before and after, time, cost, conversion, with who measured it
- Timestamps for the best moments, so quotes can be checked

**For news analysis, gather:**

- Which two to four items to cover
- The user's initial take or thesis
- The intended strategic framing
- The audience segment this issue serves

**For both, ask:**

- Who is the reader? (A role and a decision, not "everyone in the field.")
- What is the non-obvious insight here?
- What can the reader execute this week?
- What makes this worth paying for, compared with the free version of the same topic?

If the user hands over only a transcript, do the extraction yourself, then confirm the thesis with the user before writing. A wrong thesis wastes the whole draft.

## Step 3: Extract and structure the insights

### From an episode, interview or transcript

Extract the execution:

- The exact steps shown in any demo
- The tools, platforms and configurations used (name the tool categories, and the specific tools if the source names them)
- Prompts or templates demonstrated, verbatim where possible
- Before and after results or metrics, with who measured them and how

Frame the strategic impact:

- Why it matters to the reader's revenue, pipeline, cost or efficiency
- Team implications: who does this work now, and who does it after
- The advantage gained, and how long it lasts before it becomes table stakes
- Time and cost, quantified from the source

Structure as **3 to 5 numbered insights**, each with:

1. **The headline** (the what)
2. **The strategic context** (why it matters)
3. **The execution** (how to do it)
4. **The evidence** (what the source showed or said)

### From news

Do not summarize. Analyze.

- Find the pattern across two or three developments that most coverage treats as separate
- Challenge the mainstream interpretation where the evidence supports it
- Spell out what each development means for the reader's function specifically

Structure as:

1. Your contrarian take (what everyone is missing)
2. Three connected insights that show the pattern
3. The strategic implications for the reader's team
4. Specific tactical responses

### Accuracy and attribution

- Quote guests accurately. Check quotes against the transcript or timestamp.
- Attribute results to who measured them. A guest's own result is "their team saw," not a law of nature.
- Label vendor claims as vendor claims.
- Never invent a number to reach a higher rung of the specificity ladder. If the source has no number, climb with context and an exact action instead, or ask the user for a real figure.
- If the guest shared anything that sounded confidential (a client name, internal revenue), check with the user before using it.

## Step 4: Write

**The formatting rule: balance prose and bullets.** Narrative in paragraphs, execution steps in bullets. Never all bullets, never all prose. The rhythm is: prose context, bullet execution, prose outcome.

**Prose for:** opening hooks and narrative, strategic context and framing, analysis and synthesis, results and implications, transitions and closing lines.

**Bullets for:** multi-step workflows, tool and configuration lists, prompts and templates, tactical action items, quick-reference instructions.

**Opening (50-75 words, prose)**

- A contrarian claim, a surprising observation, or a pattern interrupt
- Never generic context-setting
- The hook should produce a "wait, what?" reaction
- No em dashes, anywhere

**Numbered insights (100-150 words each, prose and bullets)**

- Lead with the insight in a prose paragraph
- Strategic context in prose
- Execution steps in bullets when showing how
- Results and outcomes back in prose
- No filler transitions between sections

**Tactical close (50-75 words, mixed)**

- A bridge in prose: "Why this matters:" or "The tactical shift:"
- Two or three specific actions for this week, in bullets
- One punchy closing sentence in prose that reframes the shift

**Voice checklist**

- [ ] Zero throat-clearing or preamble
- [ ] No em dashes
- [ ] No AI phrasing ("In today's landscape," "It's worth noting," "arguably")
- [ ] Short sentences for emphasis
- [ ] Numbered framework structure
- [ ] Credibility from experience, not credentials
- [ ] No jargon unless defined on first use
- [ ] No hedging ("might," "could," "potentially")
- [ ] Prose and bullets balanced

## Step 5: Apply the quality filters

Before presenting, check every section against the filters in [references/quality-filters.md](references/quality-filters.md). The short form:

**The "so what?" test**

- [ ] Specific: numbers, named tool categories, exact steps
- [ ] Actionable: executable today, not "consider"
- [ ] Quantified: time saved, cost reduced, performance gained, from the source

**The "different, not more" test**

- [ ] Offers a new framework or mental model
- [ ] Draws on real implementation experience or data
- [ ] Challenges or reframes conventional wisdom

**The cognitive monopoly test**

- [ ] Could a reader get this from a web search? (Should be no.)
- [ ] Is it based on the guest's, the source's or the user's specific experience? (Should be yes.)
- [ ] Would a competitor pay to know it? (Should be yes.)

**The actionability checklist**

- [ ] Specific tool or tool category named
- [ ] Exact steps or sequence given
- [ ] Time or cost quantified
- [ ] Success metric defined
- [ ] Failure mode identified

**The specificity ladder:** every key claim reaches Level 4 (contextualized) or Level 5 (actionable).

**The audit:** score each section 1-5 on specificity, novelty and usefulness. Publish at an average of 4.0 or higher. Any section at 2 or below gets elevated or cut.

If any section fails, revise before presenting.

## Step 6: Present for review

Share the draft with:

- Which template you used (source summary, news analysis or hybrid)
- Any assumptions you made, and any numbers or quotes that need the user's confirmation
- The quality-filter results: what makes this issue pay-worthy, and the weakest section
- Three subject line options with preview text (and offer to run them through `icp-reader-test`)

Expect to iterate on voice (too formal, not punchy enough), depth (needs more specificity), clarity (execution steps unclear) and framing (misses the reader's angle).

## Length targets

| | Source summary | News analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Total | 600-900 words | 500-800 words |
| Opening | 50-75 words | 75-100 words (opening + pattern setup) |
| Each numbered section | 100-150 words | 125-175 words |
| Close | 50-75 words | 100-150 words (synthesis + tactics) |

**Why:** short enough to read in three or four minutes, long enough for depth worth paying for, built for signal over noise.

## The standard

**"Readers won't believe this is free."** Every issue should be valuable enough that readers question why they are getting it at no cost. Not helpful tips. Intelligence that gives them an edge.

What makes an issue that valuable:

1. It saves the reader two or more hours of research or implementation
2. It provides information asymmetry: access to experience or data they do not have
3. It delivers an advantage through early or hard-won knowledge
4. It resolves confusion with a clear direction
5. It includes ready-to-run frameworks, prompts or workflows

**Forbidden patterns**

- No em dashes, ever. They are a common tell of machine-written text.
- No "In today's landscape," "It's worth noting," "arguably," "at the end of the day"
- No hedging ("might," "could," "potentially")
- No weak passives ("can be utilized" becomes "use")
- No abstract generalizations ("numerous benefits" becomes the specific benefit, with its number)

**The default voice is not:** generic thought leadership, feature descriptions, tool tutorials, news aggregation, obvious best practices, credential-based authority, or formal business writing.

**The default voice is:** experience-backed contrarian insight, numbered frameworks with tactical application, specific and quantified outcomes, short punchy sentences, wit when it comes naturally, and a conversational professional tone.

Before every issue, ask: "Would someone be genuinely surprised this is free, not just pleased?" If not, it is not ready.

## Common failure modes

| Trap | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Tutorial** | Step-by-step with no strategic context | Lead with why it matters, then how |
| **News aggregation** | A summary of what happened | Say what it means and what to do |
| **Obvious insight** | "AI can help sales teams be more efficient" | A specific framework with quantified outcomes |
| **Feature description** | What a tool can do | What problem it solves and the implementation path |
| **Hedging** | "This might help some teams" | "This returns [x] hours a week to the ops lead who builds the Monday report" |
| **Invented precision** | A number that is not in the source | Use the source's number, ask for one, or climb the ladder with context instead |
| **Transcript order** | Insights in the order the guest said them | Order by value to the reader; the best insight goes first |

## Success criteria

A successful issue:

- Delivers one to three insights the reader can act on immediately
- Includes specific, sourced metrics or outcomes
- Challenges or reframes conventional thinking
- Gives the reader a clear advantage
- Sounds like the user, not like generic machine-written content
- Passes every quality filter without a second revision
