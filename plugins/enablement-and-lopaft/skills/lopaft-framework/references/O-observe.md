# O: Observe

The step-two playbook: show what good and bad look like, and build the shared standard everything after it depends on. Deepens Level 2 (Conscious Incompetence) and moves the learner toward early Level 3.

**Use this file when:**
- Reps say "I get it but I can't do it," or practice feels aimless.
- You need an example library, call clip library, or pass/fail sample set.
- Managers score the same call differently, or reps do not trust the scores.
- You are rolling out an AI scoring or review tool and need benchmarks to calibrate it against.

---

## 1. What Observe is for, and what breaks without it

Observe is the most commonly skipped step in LOPAFT. Without it, practice becomes aimless: people try things with no benchmark to orient against.

Observe produces three things:

| Output | What it is | Failure if missing |
|---|---|---|
| A reference point | Concrete examples of excellent and poor execution | Reps cannot tell where they stand. "Good discovery" means something different to each of them. |
| A shared standard | A rubric with level descriptors and anchor examples everyone scores the same way | Feedback becomes opinion. Reps hear different things from different managers and stop trusting all of it. |
| Discrimination skill | The learner can tell good from bad on sight, including on their own work | The learner cannot self-correct during Practice or Apply, so every improvement depends on a coach being present. |

**Both good and bad are required.** A library of only great calls shows the destination but not the road. Learners need to see the specific mistakes they are likely to make, labeled, so they can catch them in themselves.

**Diagnostic from the field.** When an AI tool rollout stalls, a common root cause is that nobody saw a good and a bad output. Practice and Feedback had no standard to work against. Fix Observe before you fund another training session.

## 2. The science you are applying

| Principle | Originator | What it means for Observe |
|---|---|---|
| Observational learning (modeling) | Albert Bandura | People learn by watching models. Four processes must all work: attention, retention, reproduction, motivation. |
| Vicarious experience | Albert Bandura (self-efficacy) | Seeing a similar peer succeed raises belief that "I can do this." Peer models beat expert models for this. |
| Worked example effect | John Sweller | Novices learn more from studying worked examples than from solving problems unaided. |
| Expertise reversal | Slava Kalyuga | Worked examples help novices and can slow down advanced learners. Fade them as competence grows. |
| Contrasting cases | Schwartz and Bransford | Side-by-side cases that differ on one feature help learners notice what matters. |
| Cognitive apprenticeship: modeling | Collins, Brown and Newman | The expert makes thinking visible by narrating decisions, not just showing the result. |

### Bandura's four processes as a design checklist

| Process | Question | Design move |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | Will they notice the critical moment? | Short clips, cued with "watch for the moment when..." |
| Retention | Will they remember it? | Name the behavior; give a one-line label for each clip |
| Reproduction | Can they physically do it? | Pair every model with the Practice drill that follows |
| Motivation | Do they want to? | Show the consequence: what happened in the deal after the moment |

## 3. Design patterns

### Pattern A: The contrast pair

The core unit of an Observe library. Two short examples that differ on one behavior.

```
Behavior: ______   Rubric line: ______
Clip A (meets standard): [link] [timestamp] [60-120 sec]
Clip B (below standard): [link] [timestamp] [60-120 sec]
What is the same: ________________________________
The one difference: ______________________________
What happened next in the deal (A vs B): __________
Label (one line, memorable): _____________________
```

Rule: one behavior per pair. If two things differ, learners attribute the outcome to the wrong one.

### Pattern B: The four-level anchor set

For each rubric line, one anchor example at each level (1-4). This is what calibration uses. Library rule of thumb: start with anchors for the behaviors you will Practice in the next two weeks, not the full set.

### Pattern C: Narrated modeling (think-aloud)

A top performer or coach replays their own call and narrates their decisions: "Here I heard 'it's costing us,' so I stopped and asked how much. I was deciding whether to push or park it." Thinking is invisible on a recording. Narration makes it visible.

### Pattern D: Peer models over expert models

| Model type | Strength | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Top performer | Shows the ceiling | Defining Level 4 anchors |
| Peer at Level 3 | High self-efficacy effect: "someone like me did it" | Most of the library, especially early |
| Coaching example: before and after | Shows that improvement is possible | Reducing fear before Practice |
| Bad example from a respected rep (with permission) | Normalizes mistakes | Lowering the affective filter |

Rule: get explicit permission for every clip of a named colleague, especially for below-standard examples. Anonymize by default.

### Pattern E: Faded examples

As learners advance, move from full worked examples to partial ones they complete.

| Stage | Format |
|---|---|
| Early Level 2 | Full clip, fully annotated |
| Late Level 2 | Full clip, learner annotates, then compares to the key |
| Early Level 3 | Transcript with the critical moment removed; learner writes what should come next |
| Level 3 | Unlabeled clips; learner scores and justifies |

### Pattern F: Calibration session

Observe is also where coaches calibrate. If two managers score the same call two levels apart, the rubric or the anchors are broken. See the agenda in 5.3.

## 4. Exit criteria: ready to move to Practice

Observe's target is a learner who can see the gap clearly: deep Level 2, with the perception of early Level 3.

| Signal | Not ready | Ready for P |
|---|---|---|
| Discrimination | Cannot tell a 2 from a 3 on unlabeled clips | Scores unlabeled clips within one level of the anchor score on most items (rule of thumb: 4 of 5) |
| Explanation | "That one just sounds better" | Names the specific behavior that separates the two |
| Self-application | Scores own call higher than the coach does by 2 levels | Self-score within one level of the coach on own call |
| Motivation | "Top reps are just naturals" | "I can see what she did differently and I could try that" |
| Vocabulary | Generic praise or criticism | Uses rubric terms |

**Decision rules**
- If learners cannot discriminate after two library sessions, the contrast pairs differ on too many things. Rebuild with tighter pairs.
- If learners discriminate on others' work but overrate their own, run a self-score calibration on their own call before Practice.
- If coaches disagree with each other by more than one level on any rubric line, stop. Fix the rubric line or the anchors before any learner calibrates against it.
- Do not move to Practice until the learner can reliably recognize the target. You cannot practice toward a target you cannot see.

## 5. Instruments and templates

### 5.1 Rubric template with level descriptors

Use a 4-level scale. It forces a decision (no middle point) and maps cleanly to "below standard" versus "meets standard."

| Level | Label | Generic descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Missing | Behavior absent, or present in name only |
| 2 | Attempted | Behavior attempted; key element missing or done out of sequence |
| 3 | Meets standard | All key elements present; the buyer responds as intended |
| 4 | Exemplary | Meets standard, adapted to the buyer, and moves the deal |

Rules for writing descriptors:
- Observable from a recording or transcript. If a human cannot reliably score it from a transcript, it does not belong in the rubric.
- Six behaviors maximum per rubric. Fewer is better.
- Each level describes what you would see, not a judgment word ("good," "strong").
- Definition of done: two managers scoring the same call land within one point.

### 5.2 Library inventory template

```
Skill: ______   Rubric version: ______   Owner: ______   Review date: ______

| ID | Behavior | Level | Rep (or anon) | Permission Y/N | Clip link + timestamp | Label | Used in (O/P/F/T) |
|----|----------|-------|---------------|----------------|-----------------------|-------|-------------------|
```

Minimum viable library for a 5-behavior rubric: one contrast pair per behavior plus one full-call anchor at Level 3. Expand from Practice and Teach outputs.

### 5.3 Calibration session agenda (60 minutes)

Run with managers first, then with learners.

| Min | Block | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0-5 | Frame | "We are calibrating the ruler, not grading anyone." |
| 5-15 | Solo score, clip 1 | Everyone scores privately against the rubric. No discussion. |
| 15-25 | Reveal and discuss | Reveal scores together. Discuss only lines where scores differ by more than one level. Each person cites the moment in the clip. |
| 25-35 | Solo score, clip 2 | Repeat with a harder clip |
| 35-45 | Reveal and discuss | Same rules |
| 45-55 | Fix the ruler | Rewrite any descriptor that caused a disagreement. Pick or replace anchors. |
| 55-60 | Record | Log agreement per rubric line. Set next calibration date. |

Cadence rule of thumb: managers calibrate before launch, then monthly, and whenever a new manager joins.

### 5.4 Learner observation worksheet

```
Clip: ______   Behavior I am watching for: ______
1. The moment it happened (or should have): [timestamp] ______
2. What the rep said or did: ______
3. How the buyer responded: ______
4. My score (1-4) and the descriptor that matches: ______
5. One thing I would steal: ______
6. One thing I do that looks like the weaker clip: ______
```

### 5.5 Discrimination check (exit test)

- 5 unlabeled clips, mixed levels, same behavior.
- Learner scores each and writes one line of justification.
- Pass: within one level of the anchor on 4 of 5, and justification names the behavior.

## 6. Observe in the AI era

| Shift | Implication for Observe |
|---|---|
| Full call coverage | Call-review tools surface candidate clips from every call, not the few a manager had time to hear. Library building drops from weeks to hours. |
| AI-generated examples | You can generate contrasting example emails, talk tracks, or simulated calls at each rubric level. Useful for coverage; less credible than real calls. |
| Comparing AI outputs is itself an Observe exercise | LOPAFT's AI role at O: compare outputs. A good versus bad prompt, an expert versus novice brief, a correct versus wrong AI call review. |
| The rubric becomes a machine input | An AI scorer applies your rubric consistently, including the parts that are wrong. Consistent is not the same as correct. |

**Calibrate the machine like you calibrate managers.** Before AI scores reach reps:
1. Score a sample of calls both ways, by humans and by the tool.
2. Publish agreement per rubric line.
3. Rewrite or drop any line where the tool and humans disagree too often. Rule of thumb: if agreement on a line would not be acceptable between two of your managers, it is not acceptable for the tool.
4. Re-check after any rubric or model change.

Shipping scores nobody trusts poisons the whole program.

**Teach discrimination of AI output.** When agents draft the email, summarize the call, and score the behavior, the scarce skill is judging the output. Build contrast pairs for the tool itself:
- An AI call review that is correct versus one that misses a key moment.
- A summary that captures what was said versus one that captures what was meant.
- A score that is right versus one that is confidently wrong because the transcript misattributed a speaker.

**Rules**
- Label AI-generated examples as generated. Learners should know whether a model is real.
- Real calls outrank generated ones for credibility. Use generated examples to fill gaps, not to replace the library.
- Never surface AI-detected weak examples of a named rep publicly.

## 7. Worked example (illustrative)

> **Program scenario (illustrative, used in all six step files).** A 12-AE mid-market team is rolling out a new discovery method and an AI call-review tool at the same time. Two frontline managers (6 AEs each), one enablement lead, one RevOps analyst. The method has five scored behaviors: **D1** Hypothesis opener, **D2** Layered problem questions, **D3** Quantified impact confirmed by the buyer, **D4** Buying process mapped, **D5** Dated next step agreed live. The AI tool scores every recorded call against D1-D5 and drafts a call review. The AEs' second skill is judging that review: catching wrong or missing scores before acting on it. Traffic light: red. Program: 10 weeks plus a day-90 check. The week-0 baseline (enablement-assess) placed most AEs at Level 1 or 2 on D3 and D4, and at Level 1 on judging AI output.

**Observe runs in weeks 1-2**, then the library keeps growing through Practice, Feedback, and Teach.

### The discovery rubric (the standard every later file uses)

| Behavior | 1 Missing | 2 Attempted | 3 Meets standard | 4 Exemplary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 Hypothesis opener | Opens with agenda or company pitch | States a generic hypothesis ("teams like yours struggle with efficiency") | States a specific hypothesis from research and asks the buyer to confirm or correct | Hypothesis is specific, invites correction, and the buyer's correction redirects the call |
| D2 Layered problem questions | Surface questions only | Asks a follow-up but moves on after the first answer | Goes at least two layers: problem, cause, consequence | Layers until the buyer states a consequence in their own words |
| D3 Quantified impact | No number | AE suggests a number; buyer does not engage | Buyer states or confirms a number for the cost of the problem | Buyer quantifies it and links it to a business goal or deadline |
| D4 Buying process | Not discussed | Asks "who else is involved?" only | Maps decision makers, steps, and timeline for this purchase | Maps the process and tests it against how a past similar purchase was approved |
| D5 Dated next step | "I'll follow up" | Next step agreed without a date or owner | Specific next step with a date, agreed live, calendar sent on the call | Next step includes a new stakeholder or a buyer-owned action |

**AI review judgment (second skill)**

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accepts the AI review as written | Spots obvious errors only when prompted | Checks each score against the transcript moment; corrects wrong scores and missed moments | Also spots where the review is accurate but misleading (right words, wrong meaning) |

### Library build

- The RevOps analyst used the AI tool to pull candidate clips for D1-D5 from the last 60 days of calls. The enablement lead and one manager selected from them. Selection was human.
- First release (week 1): 5 contrast pairs (one per behavior) and 2 full-call Level 3 anchors. All AEs in clips gave permission; below-standard clips were anonymized.
- One senior AE, already at Level 3 on D3 (see L-learn.md), recorded a narrated think-aloud of her best D3 moment.
- For the AI tool: 3 contrast pairs of AI call reviews. One where the tool credited D3 because the AE said a number the buyer never confirmed. One where it missed D4 because the buying process came up in the last two minutes after the summary cut off. One correct review as the baseline.

### Calibration

- **Managers first (week 1).** Two managers and the enablement lead scored 2 calls. D2 disagreed by two levels: one manager counted any follow-up as "layered." The D2 descriptor was rewritten to "at least two layers: problem, cause, consequence."
- **Tool against humans (week 1).** Humans and the tool scored the same 20 calls. D1, D3, and D5 agreed closely enough to ship. D2 and D4 did not. Decision: the tool's D2 and D4 scores are hidden from reps for now and shown only to managers as "suggested." Re-check at week 4.
- **AEs (week 2).** 45-minute learner calibration: 5 unlabeled clips. 9 of 12 passed the discrimination check. The 3 who did not all overrated D3 (they credited the AE's number as the buyer's). They received one extra contrast pair on that exact confusion and passed on retest.

### Exit decision

All 12 AEs could discriminate D1-D5 and spot at least one of the three planted AI review errors. Move to Practice, starting with D1 and D2 isolation (see P-practice.md).

## 8. Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Skipping O | Practice with no reference point is aimless | Build a minimum viable library before Practice starts |
| Only great examples | Learners cannot recognize their own mistakes | Contrast pairs: good and bad, one difference |
| Hour-long recordings as "examples" | Nobody finds the critical moment | 60-120 second clips with a cue and a label |
| Expert-only models | "She's a natural" lowers self-efficacy | Mostly peer models at Level 3 |
| Rubric of 12 behaviors | Unscoreable, uncalibratable | Six behaviors maximum |
| Judgment-word descriptors | "Strong discovery" means different things to each manager | Describe what you would see |
| Shipping AI scores before calibration | Reps stop trusting the tool and the program | Human versus tool agreement per line, published, before release |
| Public "worst calls" | Shame raises the affective filter | Anonymize, get permission, frame as the ruler not the grade |
| Static library | Goes stale; reps learn last year's market | Feed it from Practice, Feedback, and Teach outputs; review quarterly |
